\chapter{Divisor sums and Adams operations on polynomial Fock space}
\label{v4-arith:ado:chapter}
\label{v4-arith:gelfand-borger}

An arithmetic operation on a Fock space must be specified before it can
be compared with Frobenius, a Hecke correspondence, or a vertex operation.
Two integral substitutions are relevant here. One sums over divisors of a
mode index; the other multiplies that index. They agree on the first mode,
but their compositions differ. We compute the divisor substitutions, then
construct the Adams action and identify the structures retained by each.
The calculation concerns a polynomial oscillator representation. A map to
an arithmetic Hochschild trace class remains additional data.

\section{The two substitutions}
\label{v4-arith:ado:carriers}

Let \(R\) be a commutative unital ring and let \(S\) be a finite set of
oscillator species. Put
\[
 M_R=\bigoplus_{s\in S,\ k\ge1}R x_{s,k},\qquad
 P_R=\operatorname{Sym}_R(M_R)=R[x_{s,k}:s\in S,k\ge1].
\]
All polynomials and all sums defining an image of a generator have finite
support. The vacuum is \(1\). Particle degree assigns degree one to every
\(x_{s,k}\); weight assigns weight \(k\). The species index can label an
arithmetic place, but is independent of the integer indexing an operation.
Suppress it when there is only one species. Define unital \(R\)-algebra maps
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:eq:Tn-formula}
 T_n(x_{s,k})=\sum_{d\mid n,\ d\mid k}d\,x_{s,nk/d},
 \qquad \psi_n(x_{s,k})=x_{s,nk}\qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
The divisibility condition \(d\mid k\) restricts the sum; integrality of
\(nk/d\) already follows from \(d\mid n\). Write \(t_n=T_n|_{M_R}\)
for the underlying linear map. Thus \(T_n=\operatorname{Sym}(t_n)\).
These definitions commute with change of coefficient ring. They also
extend to any set of species as an algebraic direct limit of finite sets.
For an empty set of species all maps are the identity on \(R\).
Counterexamples below use a nonempty species set and characteristic zero,
unless a coefficient ring is specified otherwise.

\subsection{First-mode calculations}
\label{v4-arith:gb:explicit-psi-2}
For species indexed by the primes \(2\) and \(3\), the first-mode identities are
\begin{computation}
\label{v4-arith:comp:psi2-a2}
\(T_2x_{2,1}=\psi_2x_{2,1}=x_{2,2}\).
\end{computation}
\begin{computation}
\label{v4-arith:comp:psi2-a3}
\(T_2x_{3,1}=\psi_2x_{3,1}=x_{3,2}\).
\end{computation}
\begin{computation}
\label{v4-arith:comp:psi2-a2a3}
Both substitutions send \(x_{2,1}x_{3,1}\) to
\(x_{2,2}x_{3,2}\), by their polynomial product rule.
\end{computation}
These first-mode equalities do not determine the higher compositions.

\begin{example}
For a prime \(p\),
\[
 T_p x_1=x_p,\quad T_p x_p=x_{p^2}+p x_p,\quad
 T_{p^2}x_1=x_{p^2}.
\]
Consequently \(T_p^2\ne T_{p^2}\). The usual weight-two Hecke recursion
also fails: its discrepancy on \(x_1\) is
\(p(x_p-x_1)\). Neither statement is a failure of the distinct maps
\(\psi_n\), which satisfy \(\psi_m\psi_n=\psi_{mn}\) on every generator
and therefore on \(P_R\).
\end{example}

\section{Composition on a prime chain}
\label{v4-arith:ado:composition}

Fix a prime \(p\) and an integer \(u\ge1\) prime to \(p\).
The submodule with basis \(e_r=x_{up^r}\), \(r\ge0\), is invariant under
\(t_{p^a}\). Denote its restriction by \(A_a\), including \(A_0=1\).
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:prime-chain}
 A_a e_r=\sum_{i=0}^{\min(a,r)}p^i e_{r+a-i}.
\end{equation}
The indices never decrease. This boundary at \(r=0\) is responsible for
the noncommutativity.

\begin{theorem}[prime-valuation composition]
\label{v4-arith:ado:composition-theorem}
\label{v4-arith:lem:composition-law}
For \(a,b,r\ge0\), set \([v]_+=\max(v,0)\) for an integer \(v\), and put
\[
 N_{a,b}(r,t)=\mathbf1_{t\le r+b}
 \bigl[\min(b,r,t)-\max(0,t-a)+1\bigr]_+.
\]
Then, over every coefficient ring,
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:composition-formula}
 A_a A_b e_r=\sum_{t=0}^{a+b}p^tN_{a,b}(r,t)e_{r+a+b-t}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting the same expression with \(a,b\) interchanged gives the
commutator. Over \(\mathbb Z\) or a characteristic-zero field,
\(A_a\) and \(A_b\) commute precisely when \(a=b\) or \(ab=0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply \(A_b\) first. A term is specified by
\(0\le i\le\min(b,r)\), followed by
\(0\le j\le\min(a,r+b-i)\). Its coefficient is \(p^{i+j}\) and
its index is \(r+a+b-i-j\). For fixed \(t=i+j\), the second upper
bound is equivalent to \(t\le r+b\), and the remaining bounds are
\[
 \max(0,t-a)\le i\le\min(b,r,t).
\]
Counting these integers proves the formula without division.
If \(0<a<b\), take \(r=a\) and \(t=2a\). In the commutator the
coefficient of \(e_b\) is
\(-p^{2a}\min(a,b-a)\), which is nonzero over the stated rings.
The cases \(a=b\) and \(ab=0\) commute by definition; interchanging
\(a,b\) covers the remaining case.
\end{proof}

In particular,
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:first-commutator}
 [A_1,A_2]e_r=
 \begin{cases}-p^2 e_2,&r=1,\\0,&r\ne1.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For \(r\ge2\) this follows either from the formula or from the
polynomials \(U+p\) and \(U^2+pU+p^2\), where \(Ue_r=e_{r+1}\).
Direct substitution checks \(r=0,1\).

\begin{corollary}[different primes and arbitrary indices]
\label{v4-arith:ado:global-composition}
Families belonging to different primes commute. If \((m,n)=1\),
then \(T_mT_n=T_{mn}\). More generally, write
\(a_p=v_p(m)\), \(b_p=v_p(n)\), and \(r_p=v_p(k)\). On a generator,
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:global-formula}
 T_mT_n x_k=
 \sum_{(t_p)}
 \left(\prod_{p\mid mn}p^{t_p}N_{a_p,b_p}(r_p,t_p)\right)
 x_{mnk/\prod_{p\mid mn}p^{t_p}},
 \quad 0\le t_p\le a_p+b_p.
\end{equation}
Terms with zero coefficient are omitted. All nonzero terms have integral
indices. On a monomial the image is the product of these finite sums.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Multiplication or division of an index by a power of \(p\) does not change
its valuation at another prime. Thus the choices of divisors at distinct
primes are independent. The divisor formula factors \(t_n\) as the product
of the \(A_{v_p(n)}\) acting in these independent coordinates. Apply the
theorem in each coordinate and extend the resulting equality on generators
multiplicatively. For coprime \(m,n\), one of \(a_p,b_p\) vanishes at each
prime, so the product is exactly the formula for \(T_{mn}\).
\end{proof}

These formulas include coefficient exceptions. In characteristic \(p\),
\(T_{p^a}=\psi_{p^a}\), since every summand with \(i>0\) vanishes.
Over other rings a displayed commutator coefficient may vanish through
torsion. Formula~\eqref{v4-arith:ado:composition-formula}, rather than the
characteristic-zero noncommutativity assertion, determines those cases.

More generally, for \(1\le a<b\), direct expansion gives
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:boundary-commutator}
 [A_a,A_b]e_1=-p^{a+1}e_b.
\end{equation}
Indeed \(A_be_1=e_{b+1}+pe_b\), so applying \(A_a\) gives
successive coefficients \(1,2p,\ldots,2p^a,p^{a+1}\).
In the reverse order \(A_ae_1=e_{a+1}+pe_a\); since \(b\ge a+1\),
the expansion ends with \(2p^{a+1}\) instead of \(p^{a+1}\).

Over any nonzero characteristic-zero ring, the global criteria are
\[
 T_mT_n=T_{mn}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (m,n)=1,
\]
and
\[
 [T_m,T_n]=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\
 \text{for each }p,\quad a_pb_p=0\ \text{or}\ a_p=b_p.
\]
For the first necessity, on \(x_1\) a common prime contributes
\(A_aA_be_0=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(a,b)}p^je_{a+b-j}\), whereas
\(A_{a+b}e_0=e_{a+b}\). Select its \(j=1\) term and the coefficient-one
highest terms at the other primes; it cannot vanish. For the second,
if \(0<a_p<b_p\), apply the commutator to \(x_p\). At every other
prime the two orders agree on \(e_0\); selecting their highest terms
and using \eqref{v4-arith:ado:boundary-commutator} gives a nonzero
coefficient. Sufficiency follows from the independent prime factors.
Over \(\mathbb F_\ell\), both criteria have the corresponding exception
at \(p=\ell\): that prime imposes no condition. At every other prime,
the same nonzero power-of-\(p\) test proves necessity. These assertions
concern the polynomial carrier; reducing the larger Hall lattice below
is a different operation.

The interaction with the Adams shifts is equally explicit:
\[
 \psi_mT_nx_k=\sum_{d\mid n,\ d\mid k}d x_{mnk/d},\qquad
 T_n\psi_mx_k=\sum_{d\mid n,\ d\mid mk}d x_{mnk/d}.
\]
In characteristic zero these commute exactly for coprime \(m,n\).
Sufficiency follows from equality of the divisor conditions, and \(k=1\)
detects an extra positive coefficient when the indices have a common prime.

One can instead retain the prime substitutions and change the higher
operations: define \(U_n=\prod_pT_p^{v_p(n)}\). Then
\(U_mU_n=U_{mn}\), because different prime substitutions commute.
This is another monoid action, distinct from both \(T_n\) and \(\psi_n\).
Its prime-power formula is
\[
 T_p^a e_r=\sum_{j=0}^a\binom ajp^je_{r+a-j}\quad(r\ge1),\qquad
 T_p^a e_0=\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}\binom{a-1}jp^je_{a-j}\quad(a\ge1).
\]
The first is the binomial theorem for \(U+p\) on the invariant tail
\(r\ge1\); the second follows after the first step \(e_0\mapsto e_1\).
Its prime maps still fail the Frobenius tests below, so this change
repairs only the composition law.


\section{An explicit presentation of the prime-chain algebra}
\label{v4-arith:ado:presentation}

A presentation should distinguish relations in linear operators on
\(M_R\) from composition of ring maps on \(P_R\). We give a finite
coefficient test for every proposed linear relation. It describes the
entire prime-chain operator algebra, including its boundary terms.

A useful ambient realization uses \(Ue_r=e_{r+1}\) and
\(Be_0=0\), \(Be_r=e_{r-1}\) for \(r\ge1\). They satisfy \(BU=1\),
and \(U^iB^i\) projects onto the tail \(r\ge i\). Therefore
\[
 A_a=\sum_{i=0}^a p^iU^{a-i}U^iB^i
     =U^a\sum_{i=0}^a p^iB^i,
 \qquad A_{a+1}=UA_a+p^{a+1}U^{a+1}B^{a+1}.
\]
These identities locate the generated algebra inside the integral
shift-and-partial-inverse operator algebra. They do not identify the
subalgebra generated by the \(A_a\) with that whole ambient algebra.


For \(a\ge1\), put \(q_a(z)=\sum_{i=0}^a p^i z^{a-i}\).
For a word \(w=Z_{a_\ell}\cdots Z_{a_1}\), write
\(D_w=\sum_j a_j\) and \(q_w=\prod_jq_{a_j}\); the rightmost letter
acts first. For \(r,s\ge0\), define the integer
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:word-coefficient}
 B_w(r,s)=
 \sum_{\substack{0\le i_j\le a_j,\ \sum_j(a_j-i_j)=s\\
 i_j\le r+\sum_{h<j}(a_h-i_h)\ \text{for every }j}}
 p^{\sum_j i_j}.
\end{equation}
For the empty word set \(D_w=0\), \(q_w=1\), and
\(B_w(r,s)=\mathbf1_{s=0}\). Every sum is finite, and
\(B_w(r,s)=0\) if \(s>D_w\).

\begin{theorem}[finite-test presentation]
\label{v4-arith:ado:finite-presentation}
Let \(\mathcal F_R=R\langle Z_1,Z_2,\ldots\rangle\).
For a finite expression \(F=\sum_w c_w w\), put
\(D=\max_wD_w\), taking \(D=0\) for the zero expression.
Define \(I_{p,R}\) by the following explicit coefficient conditions:
\begin{align}
 &\sum_wc_wq_w(z)=0\quad\text{in }R[z],
 \label{v4-arith:ado:tail-relation}\\
 &\sum_wc_wB_w(r,s)=0\quad
 (0\le r<D,\ 0\le s\le D).
 \label{v4-arith:ado:boundary-relation}
\end{align}
Then \(I_{p,R}\) is a two-sided ideal and
\[
 R[A_1,A_2,\ldots]\ \cong\ \mathcal F_R/I_{p,R}.
\]
Here the left side is the unital \(R\)-subalgebra of
\(\operatorname{End}_R(\bigoplus_{r\ge0}Re_r)\) generated by the indicated
linear maps. This is an explicit, generally infinite presentation, not
an assertion of finite generation or a finite list of relations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Repeated use of \eqref{v4-arith:ado:prime-chain} gives
\(w(A)e_r=\sum_sB_w(r,s)e_{r+s}\). If \(r\ge D\), every intermediate
index is at least \(r\), hence every choice \(0\le i_j\le a_j\) is
allowed. In that range \(B_w(r,s)\) is the coefficient of \(z^s\) in
\(q_w(z)\). Thus \eqref{v4-arith:ado:tail-relation} says exactly that
\(F(A)e_r=0\) for all \(r\ge D\), and
\eqref{v4-arith:ado:boundary-relation} says the same for the remaining
basis vectors. These conditions are therefore exactly the kernel of
evaluation \(Z_a\mapsto A_a\). The kernel is a two-sided ideal and the
first isomorphism theorem proves the assertion. This argument works over
any commutative \(R\); all tests are finite equalities in \(R\).
\end{proof}

Equivalently, an evaluated expression has a unique form
\(q(U)+E\), where \(q\in R[z]\) and \(E\) is a finite matrix supported
in columns \(r<D\) and rows \(r\le r+s\le r+D\). Uniqueness follows
by applying the difference to arbitrarily large \(e_r\).
The symbol map onto \(R[z]\) is surjective because \(q_1=z+p\).
Its kernel consists of the finite matrices that occur in the generated
algebra. No assertion that all finite matrices occur is needed.
For \(R=\mathbb Z\), the commutator in
\eqref{v4-arith:ado:first-commutator} is a nonzero element of this kernel.

For finitely many primes, the same coefficient construction applies on
the basis indexed by their valuation vectors. Operators at different
primes commute; their word coefficients are products of
\eqref{v4-arith:ado:word-coefficient}. Tail and boundary tests in each
coordinate give an explicit presentation there as well. Every word in
the full family involves only finitely many primes.

The multiplicative extension to \(P_R\) preserves composition and is
faithful on the monoid of the generator maps: equality is tested on
\(M_R\). It does not preserve addition of operators. For example, if
\(L\) is a generator map, then
\(\operatorname{Sym}(2L)(x^2)=4(Lx)^2\), whereas
\(2\operatorname{Sym}(L)(x^2)=2(Lx)^2\).
Thus a linear relation from this presentation is not automatically a
linear relation between operators on the entire polynomial ring.

\section{Integral Adams operations and the Hall lattice}
\label{v4-arith:ado:integral-adams}
\label{v4-arith:gb:wilkerson-fails}

\begin{proposition}[Adams action on the monomial carrier]
\label{v4-arith:thm:corrected-lambda-form}
The maps \(\psi_n\) form a monoid action by ring endomorphisms on every
\(P_R\). Over \(\mathbb Z\), however, this action is not a system of
Frobenius lifts:
\[
 \psi_p(x_1)-x_1^p=x_p-x_1^p\notin pP_{\mathbb Z}.
\]
\label{v4-arith:comp:wilkerson-test}
\label{v4-arith:obs:lattice-form-failure}
The two distinct monomials remain distinct modulo \(p\).
This excludes this particular Adams action from defining a special
\(\lambda\)-ring on the monomial lattice. It does not exclude other
\(\lambda\)-structures on that ring. For example,
\(\phi_n(x_{s,k})=x_{s,k}^n\), with the identity on \(\mathbb Z\), gives
commuting Frobenius lifts and the special \(\lambda\)-structure in which
each variable is a line element.
\end{proposition}


\begin{theorem}[torsion-free Adams criterion]
\label{v4-arith:gb:wilkerson}
\label{v4-arith:thm:wilkerson}
The torsion-free criterion is the following:
a commuting system of unital ring endomorphisms with
\(\psi_m\psi_n=\psi_{mn}\), \(\psi_1=1\), and
\(\psi_p(f)\equiv f^p\pmod {pR}\) determines a unique special
\(\lambda\)-structure on a \(\mathbb Z\)-torsion-free ring.
This is Wilkerson's Proposition~1.2, as identified in Borger,
\emph{The basic geometry of Witt vectors, I}, \S1.17.
Borger's \S\S1.5--1.6 distinguish commuting operations from Frobenius
lifts. No torsion-free converse is asserted for arbitrary torsion rings.
\end{theorem}
\begin{observation}[rational coefficients]
\label{v4-arith:obs:vacuous-over-Q}
Over a \(\mathbb Q\)-algebra the congruences are vacuous, but the
commuting action is still substantive data. Its operations are determined by
\[
 \lambda_t(f)=\exp\left(\sum_{n\ge1}
             \frac{(-1)^{n-1}\psi_n(f)t^n}{n}\right).
\]

\end{observation}

\subsection{The two integral lattices}
\label{v4-arith:gb:macdonald-vs-fock-lattice}
Inside \(P_{\mathbb Q}\) for one species, define
\[
 H(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}h_rt^r
     =\exp\left(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{x_kt^k}{k}\right),\qquad
 L_{\mathbb Z}=\mathbb Z[h_1,h_2,\ldots].
\]
\begin{observation}[integral and rational lattices]
\label{v4-arith:obs:fock-macdonald-Q}
\label{v4-arith:obs:lattice-mismatch-Z}
The symmetric-function identification \(x_k=p_k\) gives
\(P_{\mathbb Z}\subsetneq L_{\mathbb Z}\) and
\(P_{\mathbb Q}=L_{\mathbb Z}\otimes\mathbb Q\).
For instance \(h_2=(x_1^2+x_2)/2\) belongs to the second lattice but
not the first. These are the Newton and Hall lattice conventions of
Macdonald, I.2, equations (2.10)--(2.14), and I.4, equations (4.5)--(4.9).
For several species take the tensor product of these integral lattices.
\end{observation}

\begin{theorem}[Adams operations on the Hall lattice]
\label{v4-arith:thm:macdonald-lattice}
The standard Adams action on symmetric functions substitutes
\((z_1,z_2,\ldots)\mapsto(z_1^n,z_2^n,\ldots)\); hence it preserves
\(L_{\mathbb Z}\) and acts on power sums by \(x_k\mapsto x_{nk}\).
For a prime \(p\), reducing symmetric polynomials in finitely many
variables modulo \(p\) proves the Frobenius congruence; passage through
stable symmetric degrees proves it on \(L_{\mathbb Z}\).
\end{theorem}


\begin{proposition}[integrality of the divisor substitutions]
\label{v4-arith:ado:hall-integrality}
Every \(T_n\) also preserves \(L_{\mathbb Z}\). Its complete-symmetric
series satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:series}
 T_n H(t)=\prod_{d\mid n}\psi_n H(t^d).
\end{equation}
Nevertheless \(T_p\) is not a Frobenius lift on this lattice:
\[
 T_ph_p=\psi_ph_p+\psi_ph_1
       \equiv h_p^p+h_1^p\not\equiv h_p^p\pmod{pL_{\mathbb Z}}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the logarithm of \(T_nH(t)\), set \(k=dj\) in the summand indexed
by \(d\mid n\). This gives
\[
 \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{T_n(x_k)t^k}{k}
 =\sum_{d\mid n}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{x_{nj}t^{dj}}{j}.
\]
Exponentiating proves \eqref{v4-arith:ado:series} in
\(P_{\mathbb Q}[[t]]\). Each factor has coefficients in
\(L_{\mathbb Z}\), so comparison of coefficients proves integrality.
For \(n=p\), the coefficient of \(t^p\) in the two factors is exactly
the displayed sum. The Frobenius congruence for \(\psi_p\) gives its
reduction, and \(h_1^p\) is nonzero in the polynomial ring
\(L_{\mathbb Z}/pL_{\mathbb Z}\).
\end{proof}

Thus passing to the Hall lattice repairs the integral Frobenius condition
for \(\psi_n\), but does not make the family \(T_n\) an Adams action.
Both lattices and their reductions remain available for \(T_n\), with
the distinct fibres specified rather than identified.

\section{Products, coproducts, and gradings}
\label{v4-arith:ado:structures}

Equip \(P_R\) with its primitive Hopf structure:
\(\Delta x_{s,k}=x_{s,k}\otimes1+1\otimes x_{s,k}\),
\(\epsilon(x_{s,k})=0\), and antipode \(x_{s,k}\mapsto-x_{s,k}\).
Both families are Hopf endomorphisms: their generator images are primitive,
and equality of algebra maps can be checked on those generators. They
preserve the unit, augmentation, and particle degree. On \(L_{\mathbb Z}\)
the same coproduct has
\(\Delta h_r=\sum_{i+j=r}h_i\otimes h_j\); its preservation follows
from the rational identity and the torsion-free integral inclusion.
This is the ordinary symmetric-function coproduct, not a constructed
arithmetic Hall correspondence.

Over a characteristic-zero field both maps are injective and, for
\(n>1\), not surjective. Indeed, on a polynomial of highest nonzero
weight \(w\), the highest-weight part of \(T_n\) is \(\psi_n\) applied
to that part and has weight \(nw\). The index substitution is injective,
so that part cannot cancel. This proves injectivity and shows that \(x_1\)
has no preimage for \(n>1\). The same argument in fact works over any
nonzero coefficient ring on the free polynomial carrier.

The Adams map carries weight \(w\) to weight \(nw\). The divisor map
carries a weight-\(w\) polynomial into weights between \(w\) and \(nw\).
It is generally inhomogeneous; already \(T_px_p=x_{p^2}+px_p\).
For \(n>1\), neither map preserves a nontrivial finite cutoff of the mode generators.
Both preserve the ideals of weights greater than \(W\), so they induce
maps on the finite-weight quotient. For finite \(S\), these quotients
have finite rank over \(R\). They also extend continuously to the weight
completion \(\prod_{w\ge0}P_{R,w}\), since an output of weight at most
\(W\) depends only on inputs of weight at most \(W\). Their integral
matrices give continuous extensions to
\(\varprojlim_j P_{\mathbb Z}/p^jP_{\mathbb Z}\) as well. This last
completion imposes finite support modulo each \(p^j\), and is not the
unrestricted product over weights.

Other symmetric-function structures have different answers. Over
\(\mathbb Q\), the internal coproduct \(\delta x_k=x_k\otimes x_k\)
is preserved by \(\psi_n\). For \(T_n\), \(n>1\), take \(k=n\):
its image contains the distinct terms \(x_{n^2}\) and \(n x_n\), and
\((T_n\otimes T_n)\delta x_n\) has cross terms absent from
\(\delta T_nx_n\). Neither family preserves the internal product
normalized by \(x_k*x_l=\mathbf1_{k=l}k x_k\), since its value on the
images of \(x_1,x_1\) is \(n x_n\), not \(x_n\), for \(n>1\).
These assertions are over characteristic zero; no nonvanishing statement
is transferred to a torsion fibre without checking its coefficient.

\section{Oscillators and vertex operations}
\label{v4-arith:ado:vertex}
\label{v4-arith:gb:is-there-lambda-VA}

On the polynomial representation set
\(a_{-k}f=x_kf\), \(a_kf=k\partial_{x_k}f\) for \(k\ge1\), and
\(a_0=0\). Then \([a_k,a_{-l}]=k\delta_{kl}\), with central element
acting as one. Species are independent. These integral identities,
and the Hall form
\(\langle x_\lambda,x_\mu\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}z_\lambda\),
where \(z_\lambda=\prod_k k^{m_k}m_k!\), fix the normalization.

The substitutions intertwine creation with creation by their generator
images. They do not intertwine the fixed annihilation operators.
For the Adams maps the exact formulas are
\[
 \psi_n a_{-k}=a_{-nk}\psi_n,\qquad
 a_{nk}\psi_n=n\psi_n a_k,\qquad
 a_j\psi_n=0\quad(n\nmid j).
\]
They follow by differentiation. If both positive and negative modes are
sent to their index multiples, their commutator is multiplied by \(n\);
this is not a central-charge-one Heisenberg map. Over \(\mathbb Q\),
sending \(a_k\) to \(a_{nk}/n\), \(a_{-k}\) to \(a_{-nk}\), and the
central element to itself does give a Heisenberg embedding, intertwined
by \(\psi_n\). It does not preserve the involution
\(a_k^*=a_{-k}\).

There is also a precise non-adjoint oscillator lift for a fixed \(T_n\).
It illustrates what extra choices of annihilation entail. Define integers
\(c_{k,i}\), in increasing order of \(i\), by
\[
 c_{k,i}=0\ (n\nmid i),\qquad
 c_{k,nj}=k\delta_{kj}
          -\sum_{\substack{d\mid n,\ d\mid j\\d>1}}d\,c_{k,nj/d}.
\]
Every term on the right has smaller index. The derivation
\(D_k^{(n)}=\sum_{i\ge1}c_{k,i}\partial_{x_i}\) is defined on every
polynomial, since only its finitely many variables contribute. By
construction
\[
 D_k^{(n)}(T_nx_j)=k\delta_{kj},\qquad
 [D_k^{(n)},\text{multiplication by }T_nx_j]=k\delta_{kj}.
\]
These derivations commute, as do the creation maps. The chain rule gives
\(D_k^{(n)}T_n=T_na_k\). Thus they give a central-charge-one oscillator
representation in polynomial endomorphisms, generally using infinite
locally finite sums of annihilation modes over \(\mathbb Q\):
\(D_k^{(n)}=\sum_i(c_{k,i}/i)a_i\). Over \(\mathbb Z\), integrality
refers to the polynomial derivation, not to its coefficients in the
\(a_i\). This is not an identification with the fixed Heisenberg
generators or their adjoints. For instance,
both \(T_n\) and \(\psi_n\) send \(x_1\) to \(x_n\), whose norm squared
is \(n\); its creation operator and its Hall adjoint have commutator
\(n\), rather than one. Therefore the adjoint-preserving assignment fails
for \(n>1\).

\begin{proposition}[translation and vertex operations]
\label{v4-arith:prop:T-n-not-VA-endo}
Over \(\mathbb Q\) or \(\mathbb C\), the usual Heisenberg vertex algebra
has translation \(D x_k=kx_{k+1}\). A vertex-algebra homomorphism preserves
translation, because \(Dv=v_{(-2)}1\), irrespective of whether it
preserves a chosen conformal vector. Here
\[
 D\psi_nx_1=nx_{n+1},\qquad \psi_nDx_1=x_{2n},
\]
and
\[
 DT_nx_1=nx_{n+1},\qquad
 T_nDx_1=x_{2n}+2\mathbf1_{2\mid n}x_n.
\]
For \(n>1\) these disagree. Equivalently, writing
\(a(z)=Y(x_1,z)\), one has
\[
 Y(x_n,z)=\frac{\partial_z^{n-1}a(z)}{(n-1)!},\qquad
 Y(x_n,z)Y(x_n,w)\sim
 \frac{(-1)^{n-1}(2n-1)!}{((n-1)!)^2(z-w)^{2n}}.
\]
This is the derivative of \(a(z)a(w)\sim(z-w)^{-2}\), and it cannot
be the image of the latter OPE for \(n>1\). Thus neither family consists
of vertex-algebra endomorphisms. Ordinary polynomial multiplication and
primitive coproduct remain preserved; they do not encode all vertex
products. When \(n=1\), both maps are the identity and all these
obstructions disappear.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[underlying monoid action on a finite vertex carrier]
\label{v4-arith:thm:HVA-category}
For a finite set of species the underlying vector space of the
Heisenberg vertex algebra, with its vacuum and maps \(\psi_n\), is a
vacuum-preserving \(\mathbb N^\times\)-module. The action is not by
vertex endomorphisms for \(n>1\). The divisor family \(T_n\) is not
such a monoid action in characteristic zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The index substitutions preserve \(1\) and obey the monoid law on
polynomial generators. The preceding translation calculation proves the
vertex assertion; \(T_p^2x_1\ne T_{p^2}x_1\) proves the last statement.
\end{proof}


\section{Hilbert completion and arithmetic energy}
\label{v4-arith:ado:hilbert}
\label{v4-arith:gb:log-p-destroys-integrality}

Over \(\mathbb C\), use the positive Hermitian Hall form with
\(\|x_{s,k}\|^2=k\), and complete to the symmetric Hilbert Fock space
\(\mathcal F=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}\mathcal F^{(j)}\), with the above
monomial factorial normalization. On the one-particle space,
\(\psi_n/\sqrt n\) is an isometry. Each summand
\(d\,\mathbf1_{d\mid k}x_{nk/d}\) of \(t_nx_k\) has operator norm
\(\sqrt{nd}\): its nonzero basis images are orthogonal and have that
constant norm ratio. Hence
\[
 \|t_n\|\le\sum_{d\mid n}\sqrt{nd}.
\]
This proves boundedness on one particle, and therefore on each fixed
particle sector after taking its symmetric tensor power.

On the full Fock space both substitutions are unbounded for \(n>1\):
\[
 \frac{\|T_nx_1^j\|}{\|x_1^j\|}
 =\frac{\|\psi_nx_1^j\|}{\|x_1^j\|}=n^{j/2}.
\]
Their polynomial-domain operators are closable. More precisely, for
\(A=t_n\) or \(A=\psi_n|_{M_{\mathbb C}}\), the closure is the direct
sum of the bounded sector maps \(\operatorname{Sym}^j(A)\), with domain
\[
 \left\{(v_j)\in\mathcal F:
        \sum_{j\ge0}\|\operatorname{Sym}^j(A)v_j\|^2<\infty\right\}.
\]
Indeed this direct sum is closed. Finite particle truncation, followed by
finite-mode polynomial approximation in each bounded sector, approximates
both a vector and its image, so the polynomial domain is a core.
For \(\psi_n\) the domain condition is exactly
\(\sum_j n^j\|v_j\|^2<\infty\).
Thus neither map is a bounded Hilbert-space isometry or a bounded
Bost--Connes isometry on this normalization.

The separately normalized substitution
\(\widetilde\psi_n(x_{s,k})=n^{-1/2}x_{s,nk}\) is a Hilbert isometry
on every particle sector and on their direct sum. It is a complex
polynomial Hopf map and a monoid action, but it changes the integral
structure and still fails the translation identity for \(n>1\).
Its existence alone supplies no Bost--Connes representation or trace
comparison.

\begin{convention}[integer weight and arithmetic energy]
\label{v4-arith:gb:two-gradings}
\label{v4-arith:conv:two-grading}
For a finite species set consisting of arithmetic primes, use:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Integer oscillator weight \(k\) on \(x_{p,k}\), extended
additively to monomials.
\item[(ii)] Arithmetic energy \(k\log p\) on \(x_{p,k}\), again extended
additively. This real grading is distinct from the integer weight.
\end{enumerate}
\end{convention}
The corresponding energy operator on monomials is
\[
 H\left(\prod_{p,k}x_{p,k}^{m_{p,k}}\right)
 =\left(\sum_{p,k}k m_{p,k}\log p\right)
   \prod_{p,k}x_{p,k}^{m_{p,k}}.
\]
\begin{observation}[energy and integral states]
\label{v4-arith:obs:logp-destroys-integrality}
The associated eigenspaces are spanned by integral monomials. The operator
\(H\) itself need not preserve their integral lattice, but the real
energy grading does not destroy that lattice. It does not rescale the
vertex-algebra conformal vector. On polynomials,
\(H\psi_n=n\psi_nH\); \(T_n\) generally mixes energies.
\end{observation}
 For one
oscillator species per arithmetic prime and a finite set \(S\), direct
summation of the occupation basis gives, for real \(\beta>0\),
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal F_S}(e^{-\beta H})
 =\prod_{p\in S}\prod_{k\ge1}(1-p^{-k\beta})^{-1}.
\]
The logarithm converges by geometric comparison for finite \(S\).
For all arithmetic primes it converges for \(\beta>1\): the logarithm
is bounded by a constant times \(\sum_p p^{-\beta}\), which converges.
The resulting product is \(\prod_{k\ge1}\zeta(k\beta)\), not
\(\zeta(\beta)\). At \(0<\beta\le1\), already the \(k=1\) factors
diverge. Multiple oscillator species change the exponents of the
factors. An infinite set of equally weighted species instead has
infinite multiplicity in weight one and no finite conformal character.

For the energy operator just defined, its self-adjoint domain is the
set of occupation expansions \(\sum_\nu c_\nu e_\nu\) with
\(\sum_\nu E(\nu)^2|c_\nu|^2<\infty\), where \(e_\nu\) are normalized
occupation vectors. The finite-support span is a core by truncation.
The trace statements concern this positive diagonal operator.
For all primes the Gibbs operator is not trace class at
\(\beta\le0\) either, since its diagonal entries do not tend to zero.
Keeping only modes \(k=1\) identifies occupation states with positive
integers \(N=\prod_pp^{m_p}\), with energy \(\log N\), and gives
\(\zeta(\beta)\) for \(\beta>1\). That subspace is not preserved by
\(T_n\) or \(\psi_n\), which send \(x_{p,1}\) to \(x_{p,n}\).

On \(\ell^2(\mathbb N_{>0})\), the different operators
\(\mu_n\delta_N=\delta_{nN}\) are commuting isometries and satisfy
\([H_{\mathrm{BC}},\mu_n]=\log(n)\mu_n\) on finite-support vectors,
where \(H_{\mathrm{BC}}\delta_N=\log N\,\delta_N\).
This follows immediately by applying both sides to \(\delta_N\).
They move \(\delta_1\) to \(\delta_n\), whereas both polynomial
substitutions fix the vacuum. Thus even the normalized Adams action
does not yield a vacuum- and energy-preserving identification with these
multiplicative shifts: its energy relation is dilation, and its vacuum
is fixed. Other representations or operations require separate comparison.


\section{The Common Algebraic Image and the Hilbert Kernel}
\label{v4-arith:ado:triangular-hilbert}

The divisor maps and the index shifts have different composition laws,
but have the same algebraic images. The difference can be expressed by
a triangular change of generators. That change ceases to be invertible
on the Hilbert completion, so polynomial injectivity must be kept
separate from injectivity of the completed operator.

\begin{proposition}[triangular factorization]
\label{v4-arith:ado:triangular-factorization}
On the polynomial carrier over a commutative ring \(R\), define
\[
 C_n(x_{s,k})=\sum_{d\mid n,\ d\mid k}d\,x_{s,k/d},
\]
and extend as a unital \(R\)-algebra map. Then \(C_n\) is an integral
triangular Hopf automorphism and
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:T-psi-C}
 T_n=\psi_n C_n,\qquad T_n(P_R)=\psi_n(P_R).
\end{equation}
For the Hall lattice \(L_{\mathbb Z}\) of one species,
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:C-H}
 C_nH(t)=\prod_{d\mid n}H(t^d).
\end{equation}
Thus \(C_n\) is also an automorphism of that integral lattice, and
\(T_n(L_{\mathbb Z})=\psi_n(L_{\mathbb Z})\).
For finitely many species the statement holds on their tensor product.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The term \(d=1\) is \(x_{s,k}\); every other term has smaller mode
index. Inductively solve for each \(x_{s,k}\) in terms of the
\(C_n(x_{s,j})\), \(j\le k\). This constructs an inverse with integer
coefficients, finite on every generator. Hence it defines an inverse
on the countable polynomial ring and after every coefficient change.
The generator images are primitive, so \(C_n\) is a Hopf map and its
inverse is a Hopf map. Applying \(\psi_n\) to those images gives the
literal divisor formula, proving \eqref{v4-arith:ado:T-psi-C}.

To prove \eqref{v4-arith:ado:C-H}, take the logarithm in
\(P_{\mathbb Q}[[t]]\), set \(k=dj\) in each divisor summand, and obtain
\[
 \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{C_n(x_k)t^k}{k}
 =\sum_{d\mid n}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{x_jt^{dj}}j.
\]
Exponentiation proves the identity. Its coefficient of \(t^r\) is
\(h_r\) plus an integral polynomial in \(h_1,\ldots,h_{r-1}\):
only the factor with \(d=1\) can contribute \(h_r\), and it does so
with coefficient one. Induction therefore constructs an integral inverse
on the Hall generators as well. The asserted image equalities follow
from surjectivity of \(C_n\) on the respective carriers.
\end{proof}

This is a change of generators, not an identification of the two
operator families. For example, \(T_p^2\ne T_{p^2}\), whereas
\(\psi_p^2=\psi_{p^2}\). Equal images do not equate their actions.

\begin{theorem}[one-particle Hilbert kernel and range]
\label{v4-arith:ado:Hilbert-kernel-range}
Over \(\mathbb C\), complete the one-particle span with
\(\|x_k\|^2=k\). The linear restriction \(c_n=C_n|_M\) extends to
a bounded surjection on this Hilbert space. The bounded divisor
operator \(t_n\) has closed range
\[
 \operatorname{Ran}(t_n)=
 \overline{\operatorname{span}}\{x_{nk}:k\ge1\}
 =\operatorname{Ran}(\psi_n|_M),
 \qquad \ker t_n=\ker c_n.
\]
For \(n>1\) that kernel is nonzero and, on the full one-species
one-particle space, infinite-dimensional.

More precisely, on a prime chain \(e_r=x_{up^r}\), \((u,p)=1\),
write \(Be_0=0\), \(Be_r=e_{r-1}\) for \(r\ge1\). For \(a\ge1\),
\[
 c_{p^a}=\sum_{j=0}^a p^jB^j,
 \qquad
 \ker c_{p^a}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}
 \left\{v_\omega=\sum_{r\ge0}(\omega/p)^r e_r:
 \omega^{a+1}=1,\ \omega\ne1\right\}.
\]
Its dimension on that chain is \(a\). For \(a=0\) the operator is the
identity with zero kernel. For \(n=1\), all the global operators are
identities.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Define \(B_d x_k=x_{k/d}\) when \(d\mid k\), and zero otherwise.
Its nonzero images are orthogonal with norm ratio \(d^{-1/2}\).
Thus \(c_n=\sum_{d\mid n}dB_d\) is bounded, with norm at most
\(\sum_{d\mid n}\sqrt d\). The identities \(B_dB_e=B_{de}\) imply
\(c_n=\prod_{p\mid n}c_{p^{v_p(n)}}\).

On the stated prime chain, \(\|e_r\|^2=up^r\), so \(B\) has norm
\(p^{-1/2}\) and the forward shift \(Ue_r=e_{r+1}\) has norm
\(p^{1/2}\), with \(BU=1\). Each displayed \(v_\omega\) is nonzero
and square-summable, since
\(\|v_\omega\|^2=u\sum_{r\ge0}p^{-r}=u/(1-p^{-1})\).
It satisfies \(Bv_\omega=(\omega/p)v_\omega\), whence
\(c_{p^a}v_\omega=(\sum_{j=0}^a\omega^j)v_\omega=0\).
Conversely a vector \(\sum_rb_re_r\) in the kernel satisfies
\(\sum_{j=0}^ap^jb_{r+j}=0\) for all \(r\ge0\).
The first \(a\) coefficients determine the rest. The \(a\) distinct
roots \(\omega/p\) give independent solutions by the Vandermonde
determinant, so these square-summable solutions exhaust the kernel.

Surjectivity also has an explicit bounded proof. For
\(\lambda=\omega/p\),
\[
 (B-\lambda)U=1-\lambda U,\qquad
 R_\lambda=U(1-\lambda U)^{-1}
\]
is a right inverse of \(B-\lambda\), since
\(\|\lambda U\|=p^{-1/2}<1\). The inverse is its norm-convergent
Neumann series. Factoring
\(c_{p^a}=p^a\prod_{\omega\ne1,\ \omega^{a+1}=1}(B-\omega/p)\)
and multiplying these right inverses in reverse order, then multiplying
by \(p^{-a}\), gives a bounded right inverse for \(c_{p^a}\). Its norm bound is independent of the
prime-free index \(u\), so the construction extends to the orthogonal
sum of all prime chains. Products over the finitely many primes
therefore make \(c_n\) surjective. Since \(\psi_n|_M\) is
\(\sqrt n\) times an isometry, the factorization \(t_n=\psi_nc_n\)
proves the range and kernel assertions.
For \(n>1\), a prime-power factor has the kernels just exhibited;
all other factors commute with it. Choosing infinitely many mutually
disjoint prime chains gives infinitely many independent vectors in
\(\ker c_n\).
\end{proof}

The inverse of \(C_n\) on polynomials is not a bounded inverse on this
Hilbert space. For example,
\[
 C_p^{-1}e_r=\sum_{j=0}^r(-p)^j e_{r-j},\qquad
 \frac{\|C_p^{-1}e_r\|^2}{\|e_r\|^2}=\sum_{j=0}^r p^j.
\]
These identities follow by cancellation of successive terms in
\((1+pB)\sum_{j=0}^r(-pB)^j e_r=e_r\).
More generally, the algebraic inverse on the finite one-particle span
is not even closable when \(n>1\). Indeed, choose a nonzero
\(v\in\ker c_n\) and finite truncations \(v_N\to v\).
Boundedness gives \(y_N=c_nv_N\to0\), whereas the polynomial inverse
satisfies \(C_n^{-1}y_N=v_N\to v\ne0\). Thus the closure of its graph
contains \((0,v)\), which cannot belong to the graph of a linear
operator. The same argument applies to the algebraic inverse of
\(t_n\) on its polynomial image.

For \(n>1\), the closed full-Fock divisor operator \(\Gamma(t_n)\)
described in Section~\ref{v4-arith:ado:hilbert} also has nonzero kernel:
any of these one-particle vectors lies in its domain and is annihilated.
The corresponding closed operator \(\Gamma(\psi_n|_M)\) is injective,
since each particle sector is a nonzero scalar multiple of an isometry.
Neither assertion contradicts injectivity of the polynomial maps.
Nor does it claim that multiplication extends to all Hilbert vectors.

\subsection{A Weighted Hilbert Threshold}
\label{v4-arith:ado:weighted-threshold}

The kernel vectors above contain arbitrarily high modes. Requiring
additional decay of their coefficients can exclude them. On a single
prime chain the exact boundary is accessible because both operators
are polynomials in a backward shift.

Fix a prime \(p\), an integer \(u\ge1\) coprime to \(p\), and an
integer \(a\ge1\). Retain \(e_r=x_{up^r}\) and
\(\|e_r\|_0^2=up^r\), and write \(D\) for their finite linear span.
Let \(E\) be the positive diagonal operator
\(Ee_r=up^r e_r\). This \(E\) is the mode-index operator on the chain;
it is distinct from the logarithmic arithmetic energy used in the
partition functions. For a real number \(s\ge0\), define
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:weighted-space}
 \mathcal H_s=
 \left\{\sum_{r\ge0}d_re_r:
   \sum_{r\ge0}(up^r)^{1+2s}|d_r|^2<\infty\right\},
 \qquad \|v\|_s=\|E^sv\|_0.
\end{equation}
Thus \(\mathcal H_s=\operatorname{Dom}(E^s)\) as a subspace of
\(\mathcal H_0\), endowed with the displayed norm. Since \(E\ge1\),
this norm is equivalent to its graph norm. Define the closed tail
\(\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_r:r\ge a\}\),
of codimension \(a\) in \(\mathcal H_s\).

\begin{theorem}[weighted kernel, range, and inverse]
\label{v4-arith:ado:weighted-inverse}
The polynomial operators \(c_{p^a}\) and \(t_{p^a}\) extend boundedly
to every \(\mathcal H_s\). Their behavior on these completions is as
follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \(0\le s<\tfrac12\), then \(c_{p^a}\) is onto
\(\mathcal H_s\), and \(t_{p^a}\) is onto
\(\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}\). Both have the same \(a\)-dimensional
kernel, spanned by
\[
 g_\zeta=\sum_{r\ge0}(\zeta/p)^r e_r,
 \qquad \zeta^{a+1}=1,\quad \zeta\ne1.
\]
Their polynomial inverses, defined on \(D\) and on
\(D\cap\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}\), respectively, are not closable.
\item If \(s=\tfrac12\), both maps are injective.
The range of \(c_{p^a}\) is dense, proper, and nonclosed in
\(\mathcal H_s\); the range of \(t_{p^a}\) is dense, proper, and
nonclosed in \(\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}\).
Their inverses on their actual ranges are closed and unbounded.
The polynomial inverses are closable, and their graph closures are
precisely these inverses.
\item If \(s>\tfrac12\), then \(c_{p^a}\) is boundedly invertible
on \(\mathcal H_s\), and \(t_{p^a}\) is a bounded isomorphism from
\(\mathcal H_s\) onto \(\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}\).
These bounded inverses extend the polynomial inverses.
\end{enumerate}
For \(a=0\), both maps are the identity for every \(s\ge0\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put \(b=p^{s+1/2}\), \(q=p^{1/2-s}\), and
\(Q_a(z)=1+z+\cdots+z^a\). The vectors
\[
 f_r=\frac{e_r}{u^{s+1/2}b^r},\qquad r\ge0,
\]
form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathcal H_s\). Let \(Vf_r=f_{r+1}\)
and \(L=V^*\). The raw forward shift on the \(e_r\) is \(bV\),
and the raw backward shift satisfies \(pB=qL\). Consequently
\begin{equation}
\label{v4-arith:ado:weighted-shift-model}
 c_{p^a}=Q_a(qL),\qquad
 t_{p^a}=b^a V^aQ_a(qL).
\end{equation}
This proves boundedness, with norm bounds
\(\sum_{j=0}^aq^j\) and \(b^a\sum_{j=0}^aq^j\), respectively.
The isometry \(V^a\) identifies \(\mathcal H_s\) with its closed tail,
so it suffices to analyze \(Q_a(qL)\).

Suppose first that \(q>1\). For
\(v=\sum_{r\ge0}\gamma_rf_r\), the kernel equation is
\(\sum_{j=0}^aq^j\gamma_{r+j}=0\) for every \(r\ge0\).
Its characteristic roots are the distinct numbers \(\zeta/q\),
where \(\zeta^{a+1}=1\) and \(\zeta\ne1\).
The first \(a\) coefficients determine all others, and the
Vandermonde determinant shows that the corresponding geometric
sequences form a basis of the solution space. All belong to
\(\ell^2\), since \(q>1\). In raw coordinates these are the displayed
\(g_\zeta\), with
\[
 \|g_\zeta\|_s^2=
 \frac{u^{1+2s}}{1-p^{2s-1}}.
\]
For each root, \(L-\zeta/q\) has the bounded right inverse
\(V(I-(\zeta/q)V)^{-1}\), where the inverse is the norm-convergent
Neumann series. Since
\(Q_a(qL)=q^a\prod_\zeta(L-\zeta/q)\), a bounded right inverse
\(G\) of \(c_{p^a}\) is \(q^{-a}\) times the product of these right
inverses in reverse order. Then
\[
 t_{p^a}\bigl(b^{-a}G L^a\bigr)=V^aL^a,
\]
which is the orthogonal projection onto the tail. This proves both
surjectivity statements. Finite truncations of any nonzero
\(g_\zeta\) give the nonclosability argument above, now in
\(\mathcal H_s\), for both polynomial inverses.

At \(q=1\), the same recurrence has solutions that are linear
combinations of the periodic sequences \(\zeta^r\).
A nonzero periodic sequence is not square-summable, so the kernel
is zero. The constant coefficient of \(Q_a\) is one. Its formal
reciprocal therefore acts on \(D\), because \(L\) is locally
nilpotent there. Thus \(c_{p^a}(D)=D\), and its range is dense.
Likewise \(t_{p^a}(D)=D\cap\mathcal H_{s,\ge a}\).
To see that the range is not closed, fix a root \(\zeta\ne1\) and
let
\[
 v_N=N^{-1/2}\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}\zeta^r f_r,\qquad N>a.
\]
Its norm is one. The first \(N-a\) coefficients of
\(Q_a(L)v_N\) vanish, and the remaining at most \(a\) coefficients
have absolute value at most \((a+1)/\sqrt N\). Hence
\[
 \|Q_a(L)v_N\|_s\le\frac{\sqrt a\,(a+1)}{\sqrt N}\longrightarrow0.
\]
An injective bounded operator with closed range is bounded below:
its inverse on that range is bounded by the bounded inverse theorem.
The displayed sequence rules this out, and therefore the dense range
is both proper and nonclosed. Multiplication by \(b^aV^a\) gives
the same assertion within the tail for \(t_{p^a}\).

An injective bounded operator has a closed inverse on its range,
since interchanging the two coordinates of its closed graph preserves
closedness. These inverses are unbounded by the preceding sequence.
Their polynomial restrictions have those full inverse graphs as their
closures: for any \(x\in\mathcal H_s\), choose \(x_N\in D\) with
\(x_N\to x\). Boundedness gives \(c_{p^a}x_N\to c_{p^a}x\), so
\((c_{p^a}x_N,x_N)\to(c_{p^a}x,x)\); the same argument applies to
\(t_{p^a}\). This also proves closability of the restrictions.

Finally suppose \(q<1\). The polynomial identity
\((1-z)Q_a(z)=1-z^{a+1}\) gives
\[
 c_{p^a}^{-1}=(I-qL)\bigl(I-(qL)^{a+1}\bigr)^{-1},
 \qquad
 \|c_{p^a}^{-1}\|\le\frac{1+q}{1-q^{a+1}}.
\]
The inverse is defined by a norm-convergent Neumann series. On the
tail the inverse of \(t_{p^a}\) is
\(c_{p^a}^{-1}b^{-a}L^a\). Both inverses agree on finite vectors
with the locally finite polynomial inverses, as follows either from
the same formulas or from uniqueness of the inverse.
\end{proof}

All these bounded maps preserve the common finite domain \(D\),
which is a core for them in each \(\mathcal H_s\). The polynomial
maps have not changed. Their completed kernels and the possible
extensions of their inverses depend on the norm: for \(s\ge\tfrac12\)
the vectors \(g_\zeta\) no longer belong to the carrier. This change
of domain neither removes the kernel on \(\mathcal H_0\) nor
identifies these weighted spaces with an arithmetic realization.

\section{The arithmetic comparison problem}
\label{v4-arith:ado:arithmetic-boundary}
\label{v4-arith:gb:ring-vs-module}
\label{v4-arith:gb:descent-bost-connes}

\begin{remark}[local arithmetic structures]
\label{v4-arith:cor:psi-ring-hecke}
There are now two explicit local inputs: the integral divisor
substitutions with the composition law
\eqref{v4-arith:ado:global-formula}, and the honest Adams action on
\(P_{\mathbb Q}\) with its Frobenius-compatible Hall lattice
\(L_{\mathbb Z}\). Neither is a vertex action. On the polynomial
underlying vector space, \(\psi_n\) gives an
\(\mathbb N^{\times}\)-module; this terminology records only the monoid
action, not a separate notion of a \(\Lambda\)-module or a trace theory.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}[module with multiplicative-monoid action]
\label{v4-arith:def:lambda-module}
An \(\mathbb N^\times\)-module is a module with endomorphisms
\(\psi_n\) satisfying \(\psi_1=1\) and \(\psi_m\psi_n=\psi_{mn}\).
This specifies no ring product or Frobenius congruence.
\end{definition}


\begin{question}[arithmetic realization]
\label{v4-arith:thm:bost-connes-descent}
An arithmetic realization must specify its native carrier, integral
form, operations, and comparison maps. Transporting \(\psi_n\) to a
Hochschild trace class requires an actual intertwining map and the
operations on that class; a polynomial presentation of another object
does not supply them. If the proposed operations are instead \(T_n\),
their same-prime noncommutativity must be respected. In characteristic
zero they cannot be identified by an injective intertwiner with commuting
Adams operations or a commuting semigroup of Bost--Connes isometries.
The commutator \eqref{v4-arith:ado:first-commutator} is already an
obstruction. A quotient or restricted subspace would require a specified
map and a new calculation of its kernel and preserved operations.
\end{question}


\begin{remark}[integral descent and operator comparison]
\label{v4-arith:rem:three-level-structure}
Borger's descent interpretation concerns \(\Lambda\)-structures and their
forgetful functor, not an automatic tensor product with a field of one
element. See \emph{Lambda-rings and the field with one element},
\S\S1.1--1.2. A Bost--Connes comparison additionally requires a
\(C^*\)-representation, adjoints, time evolution, and trace or state
intertwiners. Its standard \(\zeta(\beta)\) partition function cannot be
inferred from the full oscillator partition function above. The arithmetic
trace comparison and the choice of an integral realization therefore
remain specific construction problems; the local polynomial calculations
neither settle nor rule out all their possible solutions.
\end{remark}
